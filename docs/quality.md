# Video quality and resource controls

The Windows publisher has independent resolution, frame-rate, content-profile and bitrate controls. Browser camera/screen publication remains the transport baseline with its earlier VP8 defaults; these hardware quality controls apply to native screen/window sharing. The browser viewer displays actual received width/height, decoded FPS and incoming video Mbps for each watched stream.

## Choosing a mode

Resolution choices are 720p, 1080p, 1440p, 4K and Source. Each preserves source aspect ratio, rounds down to even dimensions, and never scales above its captured source. The 4K ceiling is 3840×2160, or 2160×3840 in portrait orientation. Ultrawide sources fit inside that envelope. Source means the bounded dimensions evaluated at capture start. A source resize does not silently change the active output; shrinking below its selected dimensions stops sharing with an explanation. The negotiated capture dimensions are checked before publication and during capture. Window enumeration uses visible DWM bounds instead of invisible resize margins, matching WGC capture.

Frame-rate choices are 15, 24, 30, 60, 90 and 120 fps. Resolution options above the source are disabled. The selected hardware factory's NV12/D3D11 sink caps must accept the dimensions/FPS before capture starts. Format negotiation is not a throughput certificate: a mode may be negotiable but exceed a particular GPU's real-time capacity. There is no silent CPU encoder or automatic codec substitution.

| Bitrate mode | Behavior                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Auto         | Recalculates from actual dimensions, FPS, HEVC/H.264 and Games/motion or Text/detail profile. Rounds up to whole Mbps and stays within 3–35 Mbps. |
| Manual       | Presets at 3, 5, 8, 12, 16, 20, 25, 35, 50, 75 and 100 Mbps, plus a whole-Mbps input within that range. Editing it preserves resolution and FPS.  |

Auto uses starting estimates of 0.10 bits/pixel/frame for HEVC motion and 0.06 for HEVC detail; H.264 uses 0.15 and 0.09 respectively. These are recommendations, not guarantees about visual quality. The 35 Mbps Auto ceiling is a conservative default for this private group: 20 simultaneous subscriptions would request about 700 Mbps of video before overhead. Manual 100 Mbps remains available for fewer/high-quality subscriptions. Five streams sent to four viewers each at 100 Mbps would require about 2 Gbps outbound, exceeding the VPS's 1 Gbps port despite unlimited monthly transfer. Auto is not a global server shaper; multiple rooms or extra watchers still need a deployment-wide budget. Production resource/shaping configuration is Phase 8.

## Applying settings

Bitrate/profile-only edits use Apply settings and update the existing encoder cap. The same RTC publication, video resolution/FPS, audio capture and preview clock remain active. Increasing the requested cap does not promise an immediate increase in network delivery. Source, codec, resolution, FPS or audio changes validate first and then briefly stop/restart capture/publication. Friends choose Watch again after that new publication. Invalid edits leave the running stream intact. Stop remains available during acquisition/publication/reconfiguration and cancels queued work as well as active capture.

The RTC sender has a stable 100 Mbps ceiling so a live change can raise the requested cap without replacing the SDK sender or widening a native SDK patch. The GPU encoder separately obeys the user's smaller requested cap and sampled congestion feedback. User-requested reductions and the first feedback apply immediately; subsequent feedback writes are coalesced to at most once a second. Live requested increases ramp in steps of 25% of the current target, with a minimum 0.5 Mbps step, until reaching the requested/feedback ceiling. A fresh keyframe accompanies a user bitrate edit so codec-header changes do not depend on old queued references. This reduces bursts into a subscriber's existing bandwidth estimate and avoids needless hardware reconfiguration on small frequent BWE changes, especially on the [pinned AMF implementation](https://github.com/GStreamer/gstreamer/blob/1.28.8/subprojects/gst-plugins-bad/sys/amfcodec/gstamfh265enc.cpp). Congestion may temporarily lower effective bitrate below the 3 Mbps preset minimum; it does not rewrite the requested value, resolution or FPS. This is a sampled encoder target, not a per-packet bandwidth limiter.

Raw GPU buffers pass through a drop-only frame-rate limiter and pipeline-clock synchronization before encoding, preserving their D3D11 memory. A high-refresh monitor cannot quietly drive the encoder above its chosen cadence. Small startup queues can affect very short output-rate averages; stable received-frame windows remain the acceptance measurement.

## Understanding the measurements

The native UI distinguishes requested dimensions/FPS/bitrate, encoded output FPS/Mbps, effective encoder target, and actual outgoing video RTP Mbps/retransmissions to the SFU. The collector samples local aggregate RTC counters once a second with a bounded timeout; raw SDP, ICE credentials, candidates and room authority never enter UI snapshots. These sender facts do not prove what a friend decodes.

Watched browser tiles independently measure received video Mbps and decoded FPS from counter differences, with received dimensions. Unwatched streams are not sampled or subscribed. Counter/SID replacement resets the baseline, and Stop/Leave clear samples and timers. Different capture, encoder, sender and receiver rates are expected during startup, loss, congestion or decoder pressure. Encoded FPS does not prove unique scene changes. A five-second decoded-frame window is more useful for qualification than one instantaneous RTC FPS label.

The room's Playback buffer control defaults to **Smooth**, a 300 ms playout-buffer preference for both watched video and audio. On the Brazil–Canada hop this gives retransmissions time to repair missing packets before their frames are discarded. **Low delay** requests zero additional preferred buffering and favors immediacy; it can be less smooth on distant/lossy paths. This uses the [public LiveKit playout-delay API](https://docs.livekit.io/reference/client-sdk-js/classes/RemoteVideoTrack.html#setplayoutdelay), with no SDP rewriting or receiver internals. The browser decides the actual delay and older browsers may ignore the hint. The preference stays in memory, survives a media reconnect, and never enables an unwatched subscription. Buffer preference is not a measurement of capture-to-display latency.
